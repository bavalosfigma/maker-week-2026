# Make What You Know

Originality has less to do with invention than with attention. The ideas that feel "new" in the studio are usually old acquaintances from the rest of your life—observations, errands, street corners, catalogs, textures you handled and filed away without ceremony. The work isn't to conjure novelty from thin air; it's to research the world you already inhabit and let it speak through your tools.

There's a consoling corollary to "there are no new ideas." If that's true, distinctiveness comes from how closely and honestly you study your sources. You don't need to reach further; you need to look harder. Research is how you turn familiarity into form.

## SLT: From Noticing to Notes

In a sound-led practice, the bridge from research to output can be formalized as SLT—Sense-to-Listening Translation. You gather what you know—materials, routines, places, brands, weather—and ask not "What does this look like?" but "How does this behave?" Then you re-speak that behavior in sound.

This is not documentary. It's structural empathy. A linen shirt suggests a certain envelope (air in the attack, softness on release), a concrete stairwell proposes a decay time and midrange profile, a morning commute offers pulse and density by minute. The translation works because you've done the homework: you can name the fibers, the surfaces, the tempos of a Tuesday.

Research here is small and continuous:
- Field notes that prefer nouns over adjectives.
- Short recordings gathered on the way to somewhere else.
- Photographs annotated with material, scale, light, and use.
- Timelines—what actually happens over ten minutes in a place, not what you remember.

You accumulate enough of this and the piece starts writing itself. The only rule is to be specific.

## Precedents and Proof

Art history is unembarrassed about research. Erik Satie's furniture music set out to study and serve the room; Brian Eno framed ambience as a function first and a style second; Luc Ferrari's near-silent field works made daily life the material, not the backdrop. Acoustic ecologists mapped cities by ear before "soundscape" became a plug-in preset.

Design culture does this as policy. Material libraries, ethnographies, use audits—methods for observing the world without rushing to conclusions. Good work reads like a footnote to something true.

## The Muji Lesson

Consider the quiet design of in-store ambience for a minimal retailer like Muji. The brief is not spectacle. It's usefulness: music that supports browsing, lowers friction, and reflects material honesty. Over the years, their BGM releases and shop soundscapes have leaned on small ensembles, gentle tempos, and unobtrusive texture—pieces that read as environment, not event. The sound feels researched: tuned to time of day, season, and the literal inventory—wood, paper, glass, cotton.

What's instructive isn't the genre; it's the posture. You make something functional and durable by paying attention to how people actually move through space, what materials absorb or reflect, what tempos let a hand linger on a shelf without hurrying. You compose with constraints that belong to the place.

An SLT approach to a "Muji-like" brief might proceed as follows. Start with a material audit: cotton, oak, kraft paper, brushed metal, uncoated board. Translate each into behavior before timbre. Cotton suggests soft attack and gentle compression; oak suggests low-mid body and slow decay; paper suggests dry transients with little tail; brushed metal suggests a narrow-band shimmer kept at the edges. Tempo lives between 60 and 90 BPM, with patterns that prefer repetition over variation. Dynamics stay within a narrow band. Harmony favors open intervals—add9s and 6ths—more air than resolution. Field sound is not garnish; it's grout: low HVAC, distant footsteps, a register beep stretched into a polite chime. The point is not to imitate a brand, but to model how careful observation can produce music that behaves like the space it occupies.

## Research As Studio Practice

Research is not a preface. It is a loop you keep running.

Walk audits. Choose a route you take often. Record five minutes at three times of day. Log the spectral center of each recording and the density of events per minute. That becomes your register map and your arrangement grid.

Material inventories. Empty your bag or desk. Name each object's material and finish. Assign each a sound parameter. Build a piece using only those assignments. When a choice feels arbitrary, return to the object and ask a better question about it.

Temporal diaries. Track a routine—a coffee line, a train wait, a grocery run. Note dwell times and interruptions. Use that timeline as the song form. Resist the urge to "improve" the sequence. If nothing happens for 45 seconds, make that nothing articulate: silence as a decision, not a lack.

Micro-interviews. Ask two people how they experience the place you're scoring. Don't ask "what music should play here?" Ask what they notice first, what they ignore, when they slow down. Translate answers into dynamics and density, not lyrics.

## Showing Your Sources (Literally)

If the origin is observation, don't hide it. Make the research audible and visible.

Show the inspiration next to the outcome. A split-screen where the left is your source footage (the aisle, the stairwell, the morning street) and the right is your session responding in real time. Caption the cause-and-effect plainly: "Paper packaging → dry transients, no reverb," "Morning crowd thins → subtractive arrangement at 1:20."

Publish a legend. A single slide or card that maps materials, times, and behaviors to sonic choices. This isn't academic theater; it's part of the work. When the mapping is shared, listeners hear more, not less.

Credit the world. If a place or a person's routine shaped your piece, say so. Research has provenance just like samples do.

## Avoiding Pastiche

"Make what you know" does not mean "copy what you like." Pastiche happens when you skip the research and borrow surfaces instead of structures. Three safeguards help:

- Verify with behavior. If your track borrows the sheen of a reference but ignores its function, you're decorating. Ask what job the reference was doing and reproduce that job in your own context.

- Reduce before you render. If you can't summarize your source as five behaviors (pace, density, material absorption, event frequency, social temperature), you're not ready to translate. The summary prevents genre drift.

- Localize. Replace every imported element with a local equivalent before release. If you used a reference field recording to prototype, re-record it in your own space. If a palette came from someone else's store, rebuild it from your own materials.

## Why This Works

Research narrows the infinity of choices to a humane set of constraints. Attention gives you forms sturdy enough to carry feeling without theatrical scaffolding. The claim "there are no new ideas" stops being fatalistic and becomes liberating: if novelty is a recombination of known parts, then the quality of your combinations depends on the precision of your noticing.

Work made from research also ages better. It is bound to conditions—materials, habits, times of day—that change slowly. Reference-chasing ages on the half-life of a playlist; observation has a longer fuse.

## Three Field-Ready Studies

The shelf study. Spend 20 minutes in a supermarket aisle. Record from three positions: endcap, middle, checkout. Build a piece whose form follows your movement. Use only sounds you can justify to the shelf (plastic wrap snare, cart-wheel hi-hat, freezer hum pad). Keep dynamics within the aisle's real-world range.

The desk etude. Inventory your workspace: wood, glass, fabric, device fans, paper. Translate each into a controllable parameter and write a 90-second piece. When in doubt, touch the material again and let your hand correct your assumption.

The commute loop. Map your commute by minute. Where does waiting outnumber motion? Where do announcements override footsteps? Sonify that ratio. Your drop happens where the world gets loud, not where the template says it should.

## Start Tonight

Choose a room you know well. Write five precise observations about how it behaves over an hour—light, traffic, temperature, voices, echoes. Map each to one parameter you can move in real time. Record a one-minute piece that keeps faith with those behaviors. Put a still or a short clip of the room next to the audio when you share it. Name two textures you discovered and add them to your lexicon.

Research is not the detour around creativity. It is the road. Make what you know, because what you know—looked at closely enough—contains more music than you can write in a lifetime.
